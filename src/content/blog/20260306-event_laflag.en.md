Officially, my first event in a school. Unofficially, a collaboration started more than a year ago that led me to talk with several families of the last years of primary and secondary school about how important it is to find the right approach to how we choose to let our children experience digital technology.

Some considerations, which were also at the center of my speech:
- **Educating to legality**: Many messaging platforms and social networks (*like Whatsapp, Instagram and TikTok*) have very clear Terms of Service, which explicitly forbid their use under 13 years of age. Telling our 11-year-old child that they cannot yet use Whatsapp does not mean being bad parents. It means primarily **having the courage to educate our children to legality**, explaining to them that it is not right to pretend that a clear and explicit prohibition does not exist, simply "because everyone does it anyway".
- An **agreement between families** makes everything more acceptable: If between families, especially within the same class, there is no agreement so that "what our children want to say to each other, they say in class, or by calling each other", it is normal that alternative communication solutions, like Whatsapp or private chats on social networks, take hold. Making case-by-case management extremely difficult. When instead as parents we answer our children with "*We and the other families have decided that it is not necessary to use the phone to send messages among classmates*", a prohibition turns into "an agreement that our parents have made". Which our children certainly will not like, but which remains much more digestible, because all the kids are in the same situation, and no one feels uniquely excluded.
- **Exclusion is a risk. But it's not as serious as it seems**: it is really rare that all parents of a class group see it the same way regarding how to give their children access to digital technology, and find a common agreement. Every family has its needs, and every parent has their vision. Inevitably there will be more permissive approaches, and less so. And the children in the "less permissive" group will feel excluded compared to the others. True, and as parents we must accept this reality. The important thing is to keep talking with them about what they really feel excluded from, about the real effects of this exclusion, if there are alternatives, if there is a "common ground" that can be found. Often, thanks to this constant dialogue, the consequences perceived by our children of this difference between groups will be greatly scaled back. But it is an approach that requires time, commitment and open-mindedness from parents.
- As parents we worry about **remote and unlikely dangers, but not about the near and concrete ones**: several parents give a smartphone to their children "to know where they are and to be able to call them if needed". And they do this leaving total and unconditional access to both the phone and the Internet. Often the fear of possible remote dangers leads us to make choices that have negative and concrete consequences every single day. It is difficult to manage this ancestral irrationality, but it must be done.
- [Using parental control tools correctly](/resources/family-link-android-guide) creates **important opportunities for dialogue between parents and children** on how to decide, together, to explore the digital world. This knowledge is not immediately available, but once Family Link is configured, the rest of the settings can be tackled gradually and iteratively.

In this context, the signing of a [Patti Digitali](https://pattidigitali.it/fai-un-patto/) between families is another very valid support tool. Furthermore, a school like La Flag that adheres to the network of [Patti Digitali Pavesi](https://www.facebook.com/p/Patti-Digitali-Pavesi-61559655394908/) provides a positive example that families can choose to follow.
